Elmiron Field Phase 3 · The consent handoff

A screen for someone who didn't ask to read it.

The MR hands their phone to a doctor mid-consultation. No account, no onboarding, five seconds of attention, and a social cost to saying no. Three structurally different variants — different information order, different reading loads, different arguments about what free consent looks like. Not recolours.

Language

Drawn in English so you can read them. They are wrong in English. The doctor's language is set per territory in Settings, and this screen above all has to ship in it. Devanagari needs a second typeface chosen and measured before these go to test.

Equal weight is the law

In all three, Allow and Decline are the same size, fill, border and type. The app's accent green appears nowhere on a consent face — the moment one option carries the brand's "go" colour the pair is weighted, and a weighted pair voids the consent.

The layout flips

Two variants turn landscape, one inverts to a dark ground. Either way the screen stops looking like the app the doctor just watched the rep use — that is the "this is for you" signal.

Type is bigger everywhere

Body runs 19–22px against the app's 16px. The doctor reads at their arm's length, across a desk, possibly without reading glasses.

A third way out

Every variant has "Give the phone back" — an exit that is not a decision. A doctor who wants no part of this must not be forced to answer.

Variant A The itemised list landscape · highest legal explicitness

The question, four facts in a row, then the pair. Everything DPDP Rule 3 asks to be itemised is on the face of the screen. Highest reading load of the three, and the easiest to defend in writing.

Variant B One sentence inverted portrait · lowest reading load

A single large sentence carries the whole notice; itemisation drops to small print underneath — present and legible, but not the thing being read. The ground inverts so the screen is unmistakably not the app the doctor just saw. Fastest to a decision; the weakest position if a regulator asks whether the notice was actually read.

Variant C Two columns landscape · test this one first

"If you agree" and "If you'd rather not", side by side, same width, same treatment. The other two make declining possible; this one makes it visible — the doctor can see, before choosing, that saying no is an ordinary outcome with described consequences rather than an absence.

Test this one first, and here is why

The failure mode that actually voids consent is not an unreadable notice. It is a doctor who understood perfectly and agreed anyway because declining felt rude with a rep standing two feet away. Variant C is the only one that spends half its screen describing what happens when you say no.

Test all three, but measure the right thing. Time-to-decision and whether they read anything are secondary. The number that matters is the decline rate. If a variant produces near-total consent that is not a good result — it is evidence the screen is applying pressure. A healthy consent screen produces real refusals.

Decisions on this screen
Neither button is primary

Both are white with a 2px ink border. Ink on white measures 16.25 for both, identically. The accent green appears nowhere on a consent face.

The rep is named

"Rahul More, Elmiron" is on every variant. A doctor asked to consent by a company logo is being asked by nobody; asked by a person standing there, they know who to tell if they change their mind.

"Not to assess you"

The unspoken fear is prescriber surveillance. Saying plainly that the rep is the one being reviewed is the single line most likely to move the decline rate — and it is true, per the pipeline.

No progress dots, no branding wash

Anything that makes this feel like step 2 of 4 implies the doctor is inside a process with an expected end. They are being asked one question, once.

Open, and it needs counsel not design

Whether a tap by a doctor on a rep's phone is a durable consent record, what the notice version history has to prove, and whether "tell Rahul" is a sufficient withdrawal route under s.6(4) — none of those are answerable from the design side. They should go to Indian counsel with these three screens attached, before the doctor test rather than after.
